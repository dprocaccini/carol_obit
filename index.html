Carol Ann Cole, was born an only child in the rural farm town of Corry, Pennsylvania. After graduating from University of Pittsburgh she got her Masters in Psychology from Temple University where she met her first husband Tony.

Growing up, she was kind of a rebel for her time. Like her mother, Isabel Cole (neé Gates) she wouldn’t take “no” for an answer. She was very gutsy and confident and believed a woman could do anything a man could do just as well if not better. One time a six foot snake made its way into the house. Even her husband was scared of that thing, but Carol shooed it out with a broom like an everyday chore.

Perhaps due to the influence of a strong bond with her dad, John Cole, a machinist, the concept of not being able to do something as a woman never really occurred to her. She loved football and despite her petite stature she even played flag football as a guard in college. When she started a sign business along with her husband, she was capable of doing everything the men did: paint, saw, silk-screening, banners and all sorts of other sign shop needs. She had a very industrious mentality. She retiled the pool patio, built a koi pond, refinished a china cabinet, put together a massive 100 gallon fish tank and as well as several other projects that required a major undertaking.

As a truly social person, she could easily make friends with anyone. She had a bubbly and vibrant personality. People knew her as being fun, bright and adventurous; wanting to make the most of life. She would often schedule activities like line dancing, going on trips or going out with friends. There were several people in her life with whom she had a unique, personal connection. She would frequently call her closest friend Mary, who remained in Pennsylvania, up until the day she passed away. Even those who she couldn’t always talk to, she adored. If she heard anything about you, she would light up with excitement.

Eventually the allure of a more exciting life would bring her to California. As expected of a woman with her academic repertoire, she was very punctual and orderly. She had a binder filled to the brim with notes and plans which was of the utmost importance to her work. Post divorce as a single mother, she would wake up every day before 6:00 am while it’s still dark outside to get ready for work, handle any of the needs of her children and make the long, hectic trip to downtown Los Angeles where she worked as a social worker for the Department of Children and Family Services.

Downtown Los Angeles also brought some dangerous times into her life. She was able to navigate those waters with the help of her second husband Rory who taught her enough martial arts to get out of the various sticky situations in which she was involved.

She worked hard for the children on her caseload for whom she cared so deeply and made every effort to ensure they received the best care possible. Not every case went the way she wanted and it was easy to tell how much that affected her emotionally, because she was invested in the well being of every child and every child was important to her.

She also cared deeply for animals. She contributed to the ASPCA. At the height of her pet ownership, she had 4 dogs, 3 cats, 2 birds and a fish tank. She even liked fictional animals. Her favorite being Yakul, a deer and companion in the animated feature film Princess Mononoke. Of course she loved her pets so much, constantly altering her approach to care to give them the best lifestyles she could. She brought up her parrot hand feeding it with a syringe as an infant. She came up with a chant to charm her yellow tang fish: “we said come on Tang, do your thang, do your thang”, which she surreptitiously adapted to all her fish regardless of the fact it no longer rhymed. Pointing this out would only elicit the (mostly) factitious response “bite the big one”.

That was her dry sense of humor. If you annoyed her, she could insult you in a way you knew she didn’t really mean it, but communicated her feelings. She’d laugh over mistakes, insults and anything ill-mannered or morbid. When she was hospitalized, having difficulty communicating, she was able to ask whether she was eating. A close friend of the family told her, “yes, you’re getting food through a tube in your nose.” Even in her weakened state, she gently lifted her hand toward the tube and jokingly asked, “is it pretty?”

Of course, her own children were paramount to her for whom she is survived: her oldest son Brennan and youngest son Devon. In the hardest of times she would make sure that they felt safe and secure. She would go to great lengths to rectify the trouble her kids would create. She was a rock for them; both nurturing and supportive.

She was so much more than what can be expressed here. The way she fought, the way she cared, her laughter, her love; everyone in her life will cherish the memories with her and the impact she had on their lives.

She passed away on September 29, 2026. Even if she never thought much of her own accomplishments, the people she touched thought the world of her and she will be deeply missed by all her family and friends.
